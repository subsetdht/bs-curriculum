# Social Studies - Grade 7

[Curriculum index](../../../curriculum-index.md) > [Grade 7](../README.md) > [Social Studies](README.md)

[Big Ideas](#big-ideas) | [Curricular Competencies](#curricular-competencies) | [Content](#content) | [Sources and rights](#sources-and-rights)

## Scope and use

**Programs:** English-language, Early French Immersion, Late French Immersion, and Francophone; shared subject expectations. The immersion policy requires parallel curriculum content [S2]. Francophone authorities, programs, and students are included in the required-areas order [S3], and the guide order identifies the social studies documents in English and in French for Francophone and immersion programs [S4].

**Document language:** English (`en-CA`), summarizing the English provincial source (`en`). Program applicability does not make this a French classroom resource or prescribe the language of instruction.

**Learning objective:** Use historical and geographical inquiry to investigate human origins and ancient societies, explain their institutions and interactions, and assess evidence, perspectives, and continuing influences.

**Prerequisites:** Experience reading maps, organizing events in time, and comparing accounts can support this work. These are helpful starting points, not additional formal entry requirements.

**Materials:** None are required to read the reference. Future activities need their own accessible sources, materials, and any relevant community permissions.

**How to use:** Read alongside [S1] and follow its elaborations through the locators. Local IDs are repository references, not official BC codes or a required sequence of civilizations to study.

**Historical scope:** The source is titled **The Ancient World to the 7th Century**. It reaches from human origins through the seventh century CE, while making comparisons with later and present-day life where useful. It is Grade 7 curriculum, not an unrestricted survey of all world history or a shared K–9 grade band.

## Big Ideas

### BI-01

The development of civilizations depended in part on the opportunities and constraints of their surroundings. Geography helps explain where settlements grew and how people responded to conditions.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, geographic conditions and the emergence of civilizations.

### BI-02

Beliefs and cultural practices developed in the ancient world can continue to shape people's lives. Studying their persistence helps connect the period with later societies without assuming that traditions remain unchanged.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, enduring religious and cultural practices and their influence.

### BI-03

As social relationships and institutions became more complex, communities developed ways to organize authority and establish laws. Systems of government can be examined as responses to the needs and tensions of those societies.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, increasingly complex societies and new systems of laws and government.

### BI-04

Specialized work and exchange created relationships between societies. These connections could encourage cooperation and interdependence, but also produce competition and conflict.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, economic specialization, trade networks, conflict, and cooperation.

## Curricular Competencies

### CC-01

Develop a focused inquiry, gather and analyze relevant information, and communicate findings or decisions fairly. Select representations that suit the purpose and audience; interpret map scales, legends, timelines, and other graphic information carefully. Make supported inferences, consider competing arguments, and cite sources consistently.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Social Studies inquiry processes and skills; inquiry, graphic communication, maps, debating, fair representation, and citation elaborations.

### CC-02

Explain why a person, place, event, or development mattered in a particular historical setting. Also examine what an account's creator chose to emphasize: the significance assigned by a narrator, mapmaker, or author is itself something to investigate.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, significance of people/places/events/developments and significance selected by creators of accounts, narratives, maps, or texts.

### CC-03

Compare multiple sources and ask whether their evidence is sufficient for a conclusion. Consider who produced a source, its purpose, audience, bias, reliability, and suitability for the question. Recognize what different sources and research methods can reveal, including artifacts, archaeological investigation, and primary or secondary accounts.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, evidence; source credibility, adequacy of evidence, source types, methodologies, and evaluation criteria.

### CC-04

Organize the past into meaningful periods, explaining both continuity and change. Identify important turning points and evaluate descriptions of progress or decline rather than using dates alone to characterize an era.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, continuity and change; characterizing periods and identifying turning points.

### CC-05

Weigh the relative influence of different causes behind a decision, action, or event. Distinguish immediate effects from longer-term consequences instead of assuming that one factor explains an entire historical change.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, cause and consequence; most influential causes and short- and long-term consequences.

### CC-06

Explain different interpretations of people, places, issues, and events, whether historical or contemporary. Compare beliefs, values, and worldviews across societies and periods, taking account of the contexts in which people understood their world.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, perspective; differing perspectives and comparison of cultures' and societies' values, worldviews, and beliefs.

### CC-07

Make reasoned ethical judgments about past decisions and actions while considering historical context. Recognize the limits of drawing direct lessons for the present from another time and place.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, ethical judgment; past events/actions and limits on direct lessons from the past.

## Content

### C-01

Investigate the anthropological origins of humans. The source's elaborations connect early African origins and migration with interactions between early humans and Neanderthals, changing stone and early metal technologies, and shifts from mobile hunting and gathering toward settled agriculture. These provide contexts for studying changes in how people lived, not a ranking of the worth of different ways of life.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, anthropological origins of humans; migration, Neanderthal interactions, technologies, and agricultural-settlement elaborations.

### C-02

Examine how people responded to climate, landforms, and available resources. Connect those responses with settlement, food production, architecture, transport, and contact between societies. Geography can create opportunities and barriers, while environmental events and human practices can affect a society's continuation or decline.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, human responses to geographic challenges and opportunities; development, settlement, adaptation, interactions, and environmental-decline elaborations.

### C-03

Compare the organization of civilizations and the factors contributing to their rise and fall. Investigate how social roles, political authority, laws, government, and economic arrangements shaped daily life and access to needs, rights, and power. Consider relationships among urban centres, specialized work, record keeping, technologies, and complex institutions.

The systems and structures studied must include **at least one indigenous to the Americas**. Comparisons may also trace selected connections between ancient institutions and present-day Canadian laws, government, or individual rights without treating them as identical.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, features/characteristics and rise/fall of civilizations; social, political, legal, governmental, and economic systems and structures, including at least one indigenous to the Americas; associated daily-life, rights, and institutions elaborations.

### C-04

Explore religions through their beginnings, central beliefs and narratives, practices, and influence on how societies understood and organized life. The study must include **at least one religion indigenous to the Americas**. Compare religious and spiritual worldviews in historical context rather than asking learners to adopt, perform, or disclose personal adherence to them.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, origins, core beliefs, narratives, practices, and influences of religions, including at least one indigenous to the Americas; worldview elaboration.

### C-05

Investigate scientific, philosophical, and technological developments and their effects on communities. Examine how knowledge and inventions changed what people could explain or do, comparing selected contributions, materials, purposes, and effects on daily life rather than producing only a list of inventions.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, scientific, philosophical, and technological developments; contributions and comparison of selected technologies.

### C-06

Study relationships among past civilizations through migration, territorial expansion, trade, peace, and conflict. Trace exchanges and adaptations of ideas, languages, beliefs, and technologies. Connect economic specialization with the goods and services people exchanged, and examine barter and monetary systems as part of understanding trade and social relationships.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, interactions and exchanges between past civilizations and cultures; cultural adaptation and language elaborations; systems and structures, trade and monetary-system elaborations.

## Elaborations and boundaries

The ancient-world scope does not prescribe a single roster of civilizations or require every example in the elaborations. Teachers select cases that address the expectations and the historical period. Examples concerning present-day Canada or ongoing traditions support comparison; they do not replace the ancient-world focus with a modern-history course.

There are **two explicit inclusion requirements**: the study of religions includes at least one indigenous to the Americas, and the study of social, political, legal, governmental, and economic systems includes at least one indigenous to the Americas. These must not disappear into optional enrichment or be satisfied by studying only Eurasian examples.

Indigenous peoples and cultures are not confined to the ancient past. Use specific, appropriate sources for First Peoples and other Indigenous societies, recognize continuing communities, and follow permissions and protocols for community histories and cultural knowledge. Do not treat different peoples' beliefs or systems as one interchangeable tradition.

Terms such as civilization, progress, and decline need contextual interpretation; they are not rankings of human worth. Likewise, geographical explanation does not remove human agency, and a present-day border should not silently stand in for an ancient society's territory.

The official elaborations at [S1] supply sample questions, activities, and source-evaluation guidance. This summary preserves the inquiry and content scope without making those examples required assignments or reproducing cultural narratives. Shared curriculum coverage also does not supply a French edition or establish language suitability for future resources.
