# Mandarin - Grade 10

[Curriculum index](../../../../curriculum-index.md) > [Grade 10](../../README.md) > [Languages](../README.md) > [Mandarin](README.md)

[Big Ideas](#big-ideas) | [Curricular Competencies](#curricular-competencies) | [Content](#content) | [Sources and rights](#sources-and-rights)

## Scope and use

**Programs:** English-language, Early French Immersion, Late French Immersion, and Francophone, as an optional additional-language course where offered. [S2] supplies common immersion secondary guidance; [S3], printed pp. 40-41, establishes parallel regular-curriculum content for immersion and Francophone programs. Together with Mandarin 10 [S1], this supports shared content, not local availability, a prescribed classroom support language, or replacement of program language arts.

**Document language:** English; the reviewed source is English. Mandarin is the language being learned, with Chinese characters and phonetic representations serving distinct roles.

**Learning objective:** Connect Mandarin sound, tone, written representation, and meaning to interpret and communicate ideas, while considering diverse Chinese community experiences and identities.

**Prerequisites:** Prior Mandarin learning and familiarity with basic tones and characters may help. These are practical suggestions, not official admission tests or proficiency criteria.

**Materials:** No special materials are required for reading this reference. It does not specify classroom equipment, software, or textbooks.

**How to use:** Read [S1]'s official wording and elaborations alongside this synthesis. The IDs identify local summaries, not ministry standards, proficiency levels, or a sequence of lessons.

The source scope is 10; the downloadable 5-10 compilation is not a single grade band. Mandarin is an available course choice, not a requirement for every learner.

## Big Ideas

### BI-01

Purposeful listening and viewing support language acquisition, and stories create opportunities to interpret and reconsider meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, intentional listening/viewing and stories.

### BI-02

Trying to communicate in Mandarin involves risk taking and perseverance, not just knowing isolated language forms.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, expression, conversation, courage, risk taking, and perseverance.

### BI-03

Learning another language creates opportunities to interact with diverse communities and encounter the many forms of their cultural expression.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, cultural expression and interaction with diverse communities.

## Curricular Competencies

### CC-01

Relate Mandarin sounds and tones to phonetic representations, Chinese characters, and meaning. Recognize how word choices affect a message, and use non-verbal information to aid interpretation.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: sounds/phonetic representation/characters/meaning, word choices, and non-verbal cues.

### CC-02

Understand key information and supporting details across speech and other texts, including viewpoints in stories. Use strategies to extend comprehension and oral or written expression, and clarify or verify meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: comprehension, story viewpoints, strategies, and clarification.

### CC-03

Exchange ideas in speech and writing and narrate connected events using time references and transitions. Choose ways of presenting information that meet the varied abilities of oneself and others.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: narration, exchange, and presentation formats.

### CC-04

Describe how regional, cultural, and linguistic practices shape identities in China and Chinese communities worldwide. Engage with Mandarin speakers and Chinese communities, consider stories' role in identity, examine worldviews through culture, and recognize First Peoples and local knowledge.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Personal and social awareness: Chinese cultural identities, community experiences, story, cultural analysis, and First Peoples perspectives.

## Content

### C-01

Understand Mandarin phonetic systems and tonal distinctions together with Chinese-character structure, meaning, and sound. Character components and radicals contribute to this study; phonetic notation does not replace characters.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, phonetic systems, tonal variations, and Chinese characters/meaning/structure; Curricular Competencies, phonetic representation elaboration on Pinyin and Zhuyin.

### C-02

Use common vocabulary and sentence patterns to ask questions, describe activities and situations, and express opinions. Communicate across past, present, and future contexts, including ways of marking previous experience rather than assuming English-style verb conjugation.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, vocabulary/sentence structures/expressions, questions, activities, opinions, and time frames.

### C-03

Recognize how common texts are shaped by format, audience, purpose, and register, and how story components organize narrative meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, elements of common texts and common story elements.

### C-04

Explore Chinese artistic expression, cultural practices in varied Chinese communities, and Chinese Canadian contributions. Connect First Peoples language and culture with oral histories, identity, and place, while considering cultural appropriation and plagiarism.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, Chinese works of art, community practices, Chinese Canadians, First Peoples perspectives, and ethics of cultural appropriation and plagiarism.

## Elaborations and boundaries

The source explicitly connects sound and tone with phonetic notation and Chinese characters. Its elaborations name both Pinyin and Zhuyin; this record does not turn those references into a prescribed sequence, character quota, or a choice of one script edition. Tone is meaning-bearing, not merely expressive intonation.

Past, present, and future are communication time frames, not a claim that Mandarin inflects verbs like English. The source does not specify an external proficiency-examination level or native-like fluency target.

Chinese communities and Mandarin-speaking people overlap without being interchangeable categories; no single cultural practice represents all Chinese people. The full elaborations remain at [S1]. First Peoples knowledge and cultural works require context and permission and are neither copied nor supplied as compulsory classroom materials here.
