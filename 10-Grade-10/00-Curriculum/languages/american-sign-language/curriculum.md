# American Sign Language (ASL) - Grade 10

[Curriculum index](../../../../curriculum-index.md) > [Grade 10](../../README.md) > [Languages](../README.md) > [American Sign Language](README.md)

## Scope and use

**Programs:** English-language, Early French Immersion, Late French Immersion, and Francophone, for students taking this optional additional-language course where offered. The immersion policy [S2] establishes common secondary guidance for both immersion entry pathways; the Graduation Policy Guide [S3], printed pp. 40-41, establishes parallel regular-curriculum content for immersion and Francophone programs. Together with the actual ASL course [S1], this supports shared course-content applicability, not local availability, a prescribed classroom support language, or substitution for program language arts.

**Document language:** English; the reviewed source is also English. ASL is the language being learned, with visual-spatial communication rather than an oral-language curriculum translated into signs.

**Learning objective:** Understand and convey increasingly connected meaning in ASL, using its visual grammar while examining Deaf communities, identity, and cultural perspectives.

**Prerequisites:** Prior ASL learning, including familiar signs and visual attention, may help. These are practical suggestions, not provincial entrance or hearing-status requirements.

**Materials:** No special materials are needed to read this reference. This document does not prescribe classroom equipment or replace accessible signed-language sources.

**How to use:** Read alongside [S1] and consult its full elaborations for the intended signing features. Local IDs are repository references, not official BC codes or a teaching sequence.

The source standards are for 10; the linked 5-10 document compilation does not make them a six-grade band.

## Big Ideas

### BI-01

Purposeful viewing supports language learning, while stories offer a way to interpret experience and reconsider meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, viewing with intent and stories.

### BI-02

Communicating in ASL develops through a willingness to attempt unfamiliar expression and continue when communication is difficult.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, expression, conversation, courage, risk taking, and perseverance.

### BI-03

Cultural expression has many forms. Learning ASL creates opportunities to connect with Deaf people and the wider Deaf world.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, cultural expression and interaction with Deaf communities.

## Curricular Competencies

### CC-01

Interpret information and supporting details in varied texts and investigate ASL media. Relate handshape, movement, and location to meaning, and recognize that sign selection and presentation can change emphasis, style, or formality.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: sign choices, meaning from texts, ASL media, and relationships among handshape, movement, and location.

### CC-02

Use visual context, non-manual information, and communication-repair strategies to understand and be understood. Respond to questions and instructions, ask for clarification, and check that meaning has been shared.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: strategies, responses, clarification, and verification of meaning.

### CC-03

Tell connected stories in ASL using time references and transitions. Exchange information in ASL sentences and in writing, and choose presentation forms that accommodate the diverse abilities of both presenter and audience.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Thinking and communicating: narration, exchange in ASL and writing, and presentation formats.

### CC-04

Identify regional ASL variation and participate in Deaf cultural experiences. Examine one's own and others' experiences and worldviews through culture, recognizing First Peoples perspectives, local knowledge, and other ways of knowing.

**Source locator:** [S1], Curricular Competencies, Personal and social awareness: regional variation, Deaf experiences, cultural perspectives, and First Peoples knowledge.

## Content

### C-01

Develop non-manual features as part of ASL meaning and grammar: facial and mouth information, head and shoulder movement, eye gaze, and spatial organization. These carry such distinctions as questions, descriptions, affect, and distance; they are not merely decoration accompanying hand signs.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, non-manual signals and elaborations on facial expression, mouth morphemes, question features, eye gaze, and distance.

### C-02

Build vocabulary and ASL sentence patterns for asking different kinds of questions, discussing situations and sequences, and expressing interests, needs, opinions, and degrees of preference. Work with topic-comment and subject-verb-object patterns, audience-appropriate register, and the ASL timeline for past, present, and future reference.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, vocabulary and ASL sentence structures, elements and register, and time frames.

### C-03

Understand narrative organization and idiomatic signing within Deaf communities, together with creative expression from Deaf culture.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, common story elements, idiomatic expressions, and creative works.

### C-04

Learn about accomplishments of D/deaf and hard-of-hearing people, including Canadians. Consider changing social perceptions, Deaf cultural life, and differing D/deaf perspectives rather than treating a hearing description as a complete cultural identity.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, contributions and accomplishments, society's perceptions over time, Deaf communities, and D/deaf perspectives.

### C-05

Explore First Peoples connections among language, histories, identity, and place, with attention to the communities whose knowledge is involved.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, First Peoples perspectives connecting language and culture, histories, identity, and place.

## Elaborations and boundaries

ASL is not signed English. The source separately expects ASL sentences and writing; it does not establish an ASL spelling system or require oral speech. Preserve its visual-spatial grammar, non-manual signals, and accessible presentation choices.

The source distinguishes cultural identification as Deaf from the broader use of deaf and uses D/deaf when discussing both. Do not assume a single identity, communication preference, or experience for everyone who is deaf or hard of hearing. Regional variation and the historical treatment of Deaf people are part of the course.

No fixed vocabulary count, certification level, or universal fluency benchmark is stated here. The full elaborations at [S1] contain signing examples and cultural contexts, not a compulsory repertoire of works. First Peoples and Deaf community stories and knowledge require appropriate attribution, context, and permission; none are reproduced.
