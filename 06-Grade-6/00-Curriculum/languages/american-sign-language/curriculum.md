# American Sign Language (ASL) - Grade 6

[Curriculum index](../../../../curriculum-index.md) > [Grade 6](../../README.md) > [Languages](../README.md) > American Sign Language (ASL)

[Big Ideas](#big-ideas) | [Curricular Competencies](#curricular-competencies) | [Content](#content) | [Sources and rights](#sources-and-rights)

## Scope and use

**Programs:** English-language, as a second-language course option. Applicability to other programs has not been established here.

**Document language:** English, as is the official source. The target language is ASL; this is not a spoken-English or written-English course.

**Learning objective:** Understand and exchange familiar signed messages while learning how ASL's visual structures and Deaf cultural contexts shape communication.

**Prerequisites:** Earlier experience with signing and attentive viewing can help; this reference sets no admission or proficiency requirement.

**Materials:** None beyond this reference and access to [S1]. Any later signed texts or media need their own accessibility, cultural-suitability, and permissions review.

**How to use:** Follow the locators to Grade 6 expectations and elaborations in [S1], not the other grades in its combined download. Local IDs identify repository summaries, not official BC codes. Consult the [Languages inventory](../README.md#requirement-and-course-choice), [S2], and [S3] for the second-language requirement, course choice, and conditional exceptions; this course is not required of every Grade 6 student.

## Big Ideas

### BI-01

Careful viewing makes meaning available through the whole signing interaction, including the face and body. Gestures can help participants clarify a message and keep an emerging conversation going.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, viewing with intent, non-verbal cues, and reciprocal communication using mime and gestures.

### BI-02

Stories support language development while offering ways to explore experience, knowledge, and identity.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, stories, acquiring language, and understanding the world.

### BI-03

Learning ASL alongside the experiences of Deaf communities develops cultural understanding, including awareness that Deaf cultural identity and deafness are not interchangeable.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, ASL language, Deaf communities, and the D/deaf elaboration.

## Curricular Competencies

### CC-01

Interpret familiar signed messages, stories, and creative works by locating their key information. Attend to handshape and sign location as well as expressive style, facial movement, and gesture, because changes in these features can change meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Thinking and communicating, style and gestures, handshape/location relationships, signed phrases, and comprehension of vocabulary, stories, and creative works.

### CC-02

Draw on context and known signing patterns to resolve unfamiliar meaning. Respond to straightforward questions and directions, and ask for clarification rather than allowing a misunderstanding to end the exchange.

**Source locator:** [S1], Thinking and communicating, language-learning strategies, responses to questions/commands/instructions, and seeking clarification.

### CC-03

Express ideas in complete ASL sentences, develop fingerspelling proficiency, and create short number stories that organize meaning through a sequence of number signs.

**Source locator:** [S1], Thinking and communicating, ASL sentences, finger spelling, and creating simple number stories.

### CC-04

Choose ways of presenting information that make communication accessible to both the signer and the audience.

**Source locator:** [S1], Thinking and communicating, presentation format suited to one's own and others' diverse abilities.

### CC-05

Develop awareness of Deaf communities and consider how culture shapes one's own and other people's experiences. Recognize First Peoples knowledge, local cultural knowledge, and different ways of knowing rather than assuming a single shared worldview.

**Source locator:** [S1], Personal and social awareness, Deaf communities, cultural lens, and First Peoples perspectives and knowledge.

## Content

### C-01

Understand ASL as a complete natural language. Its visual grammar includes classifiers and non-manual signals: handshape can represent kinds or movements of things, while facial and body features contribute grammatical and expressive meaning.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, ASL as a natural language, basic classifiers, and non-manual signals.

### C-02

Build a useful everyday signing repertoire for introducing and describing people, discussing interests and preferences, asking questions, and communicating feelings or physical states. Basic ASL structure organizes such information through topic, time, space, and contrasts.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, introduction protocols and common high-frequency signs; ASL structure elaboration on topic/time, choices, emotion, and statements/questions.

### C-03

Learn how a number story uses the ordered signs 1–5 as a framework for conveying a brief narrative.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, number story 1–5; Thinking and communicating, number stories elaboration.

### C-04

Explore Deaf and other diverse communities alongside First Peoples connections among language, histories, identity, and place. These are cultural dimensions of language learning, not background material detached from communication.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, cultural aspects of Deaf and other diverse communities and First Peoples perspectives connecting language and culture.

## Elaborations and boundaries

The classifier elaboration identifies CL:1, CL:B, CL:3, and modified CL:L. Non-manual features include question marking, mouth morphemes, head movements, and spatial contrasts; they are not merely decorative gestures. Consult [S1] for their precise scope and examples rather than treating this summary as a signing manual.

[S1] explicitly describes ASL as non-written and explains that an ASL sentence has no written form. Fingerspelling does not turn the course into written English, and a spoken-language listening requirement should not replace its viewing expectations.

The 1–5 number-story range is specific to this grade. Other elaboration examples are not a mandatory repertoire of performances or cultural works. Learning about Deaf cultures does not imply that every deaf person shares a cultural identity or communication preference. First Peoples stories and community knowledge require appropriate context and permission; a curriculum reference does not authorize recording, retelling, or adaptation.
