# Social Studies - Grade 4

[Curriculum index](../../../curriculum-index.md) > [Grade 4](../README.md) > Social Studies

[Big Ideas](#big-ideas) | [Curricular Competencies](#curricular-competencies) | [Content](#content) | [Sources and rights](#sources-and-rights)

## Scope and use

**Programs:** English-language, Early French Immersion, and Francophone; shared subject requirements under [S2].

**Document language:** English; the reviewed curriculum source is in English. Classroom-language distinctions appear in the [program overview](../README.md#choose-a-program).

**Learning objective:** Investigate First Peoples and European contact, the development of British Columbia, and the relationships among land, resources, population, power, and historical decisions.

**Prerequisites:** Experience discussing communities, maps, and different perspectives can help. This reference does not assume a shared family history or require personal disclosure.

**Materials:** None are needed to read this reference. No historical documents, images, community stories, or teaching activities are supplied.

**How to use:** The source's focus is **First Peoples and European Contact**. Read [S1] for the complete expectations, inquiry skills, and elaborations. Use local IDs to make references traceable; they are not ministry codes or a mandated chronological teaching sequence.

## Big Ideas

### BI-01

Competition for natural resources and changes in population helped reshape communities, landscapes, and economic and political power in Canada and North America.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, pursuit of natural resources and demographic shifts in power.

### BI-02

Relations between First Peoples and Europeans involved both co-operation and conflict, with consequences that continue to influence Canada.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, interactions between First Peoples and Europeans and Canada's identity.

### BI-03

British Columbia's entry into Canada arose from a distinctive regional history rather than a uniform national path.

**Source locator:** [S1], Big Ideas, British Columbia's path into Canada.

## Content

### C-01

Connect Canada's physical regions and natural resources with early relationships between First Peoples and Europeans, including exchange, alliances, co-operation, and conflict.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, early contact/trade/co-operation/conflict and physiographic features/natural resources of Canada.

### C-02

Investigate the fur trade in pre-Confederation Canada and British Columbia, and changes in First Peoples and non-First Peoples populations in pre-Confederation BC. The elaborations connect population change with disease, migration, settlement, and displacement.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, fur trade and demographic changes; associated elaborations.

### C-03

Consider how economic and political pressures shaped the colonization of British Columbia and its entry into Confederation.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, economic/political factors in colonization and Confederation; examples include trade, settlement, gold rushes, debt, boundaries, and transportation.

### C-04

Examine the consequences of colonization for First Peoples societies in BC and Canada, including effects on land, population, language, culture, and rights. Situate these broader developments alongside the histories of the local community and local First Peoples communities.

**Source locator:** [S1], Content, impacts of colonization and local community/First Peoples histories; colonization elaboration.

## Elaborations and boundaries

The source's colonization and ethical-judgment elaborations identify the **Indian Act, potlatch ban, reserve system, residential schools, and treaties** as important examples concerning First Peoples rights and early governments. Do not sanitize the harms of colonization, describe Indigenous peoples only as historical figures, or replace their accounts with a single European narrative.

The fur-trade and demographic content explicitly focuses on pre-Confederation periods. That does not limit every topic to those dates: BC's entry into Confederation, later colonial policies named in the elaborations, and continuing consequences also matter. The source is not a comprehensive chronology of all Canadian history.

Named explorers, locations, questions, and suggested presentations in the elaborations are examples, not a required person-by-person checklist. Historical perspective-taking does not mean endorsing harmful actions or presenting invented accounts as authentic Indigenous voices.

Use appropriate local sources and cultural permissions when considering community history. This reference reproduces no testimony, stories, or cultural works.
